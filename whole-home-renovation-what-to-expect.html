What to expect during a whole-home renovation

Redoing every room at once is a different kind of project. Here's how it actually unfolds — and what living through it looks like.

A kitchen remodel or a bathroom remodel has a shape you can picture: one room, one crew sequence, a few weeks of disruption in a space you can close the door on. A whole-home renovation is a different animal. Every trade is in the house at once or close to it, decisions in one room affect the next, and there's no closing the door on the mess because the mess is the whole house. Knowing the shape of it in advance is what keeps a project like this from feeling chaotic.

It starts with scope, not swatches

Before any demo happens, a whole-home project needs a real scope document — room by room, what's changing structurally versus cosmetically, and where the budget is actually going. This is also when we sort out what requires permits and what doesn't, because structural changes, electrical panel work, and plumbing relocations all trigger inspections that have to be scheduled around the build, not after it. Skipping this step is the single biggest reason whole-home projects run over budget: someone picks finishes before anyone has confirmed the wall they're standing next to can actually move.

Phase one: demo and structure

Structural work — load-bearing changes, foundation repairs, framing for new openings — has to happen first and has to happen right, because everything else gets built on top of it. This is also usually the loudest, dustiest phase, and the one where surprises show up: a previous owner's unpermitted wiring, water damage behind a wall, framing that doesn't match the original plans. A good contractor budgets contingency time and money for this phase specifically, because it's where unknowns live.

Phase two: rough-in

Once walls are open and framing is set, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC crews run their rough-in work before anything gets closed up. This is the phase where sequencing matters most — three or four trades need access to the same spaces in a specific order, and a delay in one (a permit inspection that gets rescheduled, a material that's backordered) pushes everyone behind it. This is also your last real chance to move a light switch or add an outlet before it's buried in sheetrock.

Phase three: close-up and finishes

Insulation, sheetrock, flooring, cabinetry, paint, fixtures — this is the phase that actually looks like progress, because rooms start reading as rooms again instead of framed-out shells. It's also usually the longest phase in a whole-home project, since it touches every room in the house rather than concentrating in one or two. Kitchens and bathrooms typically finish last, since they carry the most trades — cabinetry, countertops, tile, plumbing fixtures, electrical — layered in a specific order.

Living through it

The honest answer is: it depends how much of the house is torn up at once. Some owners phase the project specifically so they can stay — living in the finished half of the house while the other half is under construction — which stretches the timeline but avoids a move. Others relocate for the heaviest weeks, particularly if the kitchen and only bathroom are both offline at the same time. Either way, this should be a conversation you have with your contractor during scope planning, not something you figure out once dust is already in the air. Set expectations early for noise windows, which entrances stay usable, where contractors will stage materials, and how debris gets hauled out — a project that phases access thoughtfully is a lot more livable than one that doesn't.

What actually drives the timeline

Square footage matters less than most people expect. What actually drives a whole-home timeline is how much structural work is involved, how many wet areas (kitchens, bathrooms, laundry) are being touched, and how far material selections lag behind the schedule. Custom cabinetry, specialty tile, and made-to-order fixtures all have lead times measured in weeks, and if those orders go in late, the finish phase waits on them no matter how fast the framing went.

One contractor managing the whole sequence — rather than an owner coordinating separate trades — is what keeps a project like this on track. If you're weighing a whole-house renovation against tackling rooms one at a time, or you're ready to talk scope and phasing for your own home, a consultation is free and it's the right place to start.
