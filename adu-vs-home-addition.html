ADU vs. home addition: which is right for you?

Casita, ADU, attached addition, or sunroom — four different ways to add space that get lumped together, and shouldn't be.

"We need more space" is where almost every home addition conversation starts, but it isn't actually one decision — it's four, and they solve different problems. An attached addition, a detached ADU, a casita, and a sunroom all add square footage, but they differ in what that space is legally allowed to be, what it costs to build, and how it changes your property. Here's how to tell them apart before you fall in love with the wrong one.

Home addition: more of the house you already have

An attached addition — a primary suite bump-out, a second-story build, a kitchen expansion, a new room off the back of the house — is legally and functionally part of your existing home. It shares your foundation, roofline, and often your HVAC system, which is why the hardest part of the job isn't the new space itself, it's the seam where old meets new. Framing and roof tie-ins have to match what's already there, or the addition reads as bolted-on rather than built-in. This is the right call when what you're missing is more of what you already have — a bigger primary bedroom, a proper home office, a larger kitchen — not a separate living situation.

ADU: a second, permitted home on your lot

An accessory dwelling unit is a different animal entirely: a complete, independently permitted dwelling with its own kitchen, bathroom, and living space, built either attached to the house or as a standalone structure — often over or beside a garage. Because it's permitted as its own unit rather than as an extension of the main house, an ADU can legally be rented out, occupied long-term by a family member, or used as a genuinely separate household. That legal independence is the whole point, and it's also what adds complexity — ADU projects carry their own permitting and code requirements on top of the standard slab, framing, and utility work, since the city is approving a second dwelling, not a room addition.

Casita: a room of its own, without the full dwelling status

A casita sits in between. It's a complete small structure — its own slab, framing, roof, and finishes — built as a guest house, backyard office, studio, or pool house. Many casitas include a kitchenette and bathroom, which gives them real independence for guests or a home-based business, but they're not necessarily built or permitted as a full legal dwelling the way an ADU is. If what you need is a private space with a door of its own — not a rentable unit, not a second address — a casita usually gets you there for less complexity than a full ADU.

Sunroom: light and views, not a bedroom substitute

A sunroom solves a narrower problem: you want a room that feels like being outside, without the Texas heat, bugs, or wind. Three-season and four-season versions exist depending on how much you want it to function like fully conditioned interior space, and both tie into your existing roofline the same way an addition does. Sunrooms are the lightest-touch option of the four, but they're not a substitute for a bedroom, office, or guest suite — they're for the space between indoors and outdoors that most Texas backyards are missing.

Questions that point you to the right one

Does it need its own kitchen and bathroom? That points to an ADU or a well-equipped casita, not an attached addition. Could someone else legally live there — a renter, an adult child, a parent? That's an ADU question specifically, since that's what the permitting is built for. Is this really about needing more of your existing home — a bigger bedroom, a real office, more kitchen? That's an addition. Do you mainly want a bright, comfortable spot to sit that isn't your patio in July? That's a sunroom.

Budget and timeline scale with legal complexity, roughly in this order: sunroom, addition, casita, ADU — though lot size, existing utilities, and your specific city's permitting process all move that around. The honest answer for most homeowners only becomes clear once someone's walked the lot and heard what the space actually needs to do.
