Interior or exterior first?

When a project touches the whole property, the order you tackle it in isn't a detail — it's the decision that protects your budget and your finishes.

Plenty of homeowners come to us wanting it all — a reworked kitchen, new flooring throughout, and a backyard that finally matches. That's a great instinct, and it's usually cheaper in the long run than doing it in scattered pieces over several years. But once both the inside and the outside are on the table, a new question shows up: what gets built first? The order matters more than most people expect, and getting it backwards can mean redoing work you just paid for.

The general rule: structure and exterior envelope first

If your scope includes anything that touches the building envelope — roofing, siding, windows and doors, foundation or structural work, or an addition that changes the home's footprint — that work belongs first, before interior finishes go in. Envelope work is dusty, it's weather-exposed, and it sometimes requires opening walls from the outside in. Finish a kitchen or primary bath before a roof replacement or window swap is done, and you're gambling those brand-new finishes against a leak, a dust cloud, or a crew standing on your new floors to get equipment inside.

Then: major exterior hardscape that affects access

Next comes exterior work that changes how equipment and materials move around the property — driveway or hardscape replacement, major grading, or anything that currently doubles as the route material trucks and skid steers use to reach the backyard. If that access disappears too early, later phases get slower and more expensive because crews are hand-carrying materials instead of driving them in. This is also the point to finalize any landscaping or hardscaping plans that share a footprint with the interior work, even if the physical build happens later — so nothing gets designed twice.

Then: interior remodeling

With the envelope sealed and site access settled, interior work — kitchens, bathrooms, flooring, cabinetry, whole-home layout changes — can proceed without something outside threatening to undo it. This is also usually the right time to finalize interior paint colors and trim, since they're the last things touched before you're living in the space again.

Last: finish landscaping and outdoor living features

Pools, outdoor kitchens, patios, and planting beds go in last, for a simple reason — construction traffic is hard on finished landscaping. A pool deck poured before the interior is done risks getting scuffed, stained, or used as a staging area. Save the yard's final touches, including a new outdoor living space or pool, for the very end of the sequence, once heavy equipment has no more reason to cross the property.

Where the rule bends

This isn't a rigid formula — every property has its own constraints. A detached casita or ADU can often be built on a separate timeline from interior work in the main house, since it doesn't share structure or access. A tight budget might mean phasing the project over a year or two rather than all at once, which changes the math around what needs protecting from what. And sometimes a homeowner's timeline — a move-in date, a listing date, an event — outweighs the "ideal" order. The point isn't to follow the sequence rigidly; it's to know why it exists so you can make an informed tradeoff instead of an accidental one.

Why this is a planning conversation, not a construction one

The best time to settle sequencing is before a shovel goes in the ground, not once two crews are already scheduled and a change gets requested. That's exactly what the design phase of our design-build process is for — mapping the full scope up front, in the order it needs to happen, so nothing gets built twice and nothing new gets damaged by something old still in progress.

If you're weighing a project that touches both the inside and the outside of your home, talk to us before you pick a starting point. A free consultation is the easiest way to get the sequence right the first time.
