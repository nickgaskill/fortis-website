Pool permitting in Central Texas: what to expect

Before the first shovel goes in the ground, a new pool has to clear paperwork — HOA, city, and inspections. Here's the general shape of that process.

Homeowners planning a new pool are usually focused on shape, size, and finishes — understandably, since that's the fun part. But before any of that gets built, the project has to work its way through a permitting process that looks similar across most Central Texas jurisdictions, even though the specific forms, fees, and timelines vary by city and county. Knowing the general shape of that process ahead of time makes it far less frustrating when it's actually happening.

HOA review comes first, if you have one

If your property is in a neighborhood with a homeowners association, that review almost always happens before — or alongside — the city permit application, and it can be the slower of the two. HOAs typically want to see a site plan showing the pool's location, fencing, and any structures like a spa or outdoor kitchen, and many have their own rules about setbacks from property lines, screening from neighbors, or acceptable decking materials that go beyond what the city requires. Submitting to the HOA early, in parallel with design finalization, keeps it from becoming the bottleneck later.

Setbacks and site plan review

Every jurisdiction has rules about how close a pool, deck, or equipment pad can sit to property lines, easements, septic fields, and the house itself. Sloped lots and lots with mature trees add another layer, since grading and drainage plans often need to show how water will be routed around the new pool instead of toward the house or a neighboring property. This is where a design that looked great on paper sometimes needs to shift a few feet — better to find that out during site plan review than after excavation starts.

Utility locates and easements

Before digging, buried utility lines get marked so the excavation crew knows what to avoid. Easements — the strips of yard where a utility company has the legal right to access lines — also factor into where a pool can legally sit, even on a lot that otherwise has plenty of room. This step is usually quick, but it's not optional, and skipping it is how projects end up with expensive surprises mid-dig.

The inspection sequence

Once permitted, most pool projects move through several distinct inspections rather than one at the end. A typical sequence includes an inspection after excavation and steel placement (confirming the rebar and bond beam meet code before concrete covers them up), a plumbing and electrical inspection before decking goes down, and a final inspection that covers the completed pool, decking, and required safety barrier. Each stage has to pass before the next phase of work can proceed, which is one reason pool timelines rarely move in a straight line — a scheduling gap waiting on an inspector is normal, not a sign something's gone wrong.

Barrier and safety code

Pool safety barrier requirements — fencing height, self-latching gates, alarms on doors that open to the pool area — are one of the most consistently enforced parts of the process across Central Texas jurisdictions, because they're tied to child-safety code rather than local preference. Final inspection typically won't pass without a compliant barrier in place, so it's worth planning fencing and gate hardware early in the design rather than treating it as an afterthought once the pool itself is finished.

What actually affects your timeline

The two biggest variables aren't usually the city itself — they're whether you have an HOA to clear first, and how complete your site plan and engineering package are when you submit. A design that's still changing after submission means resubmission, and resubmission means starting the review clock over. The most reliable way to keep a permitting timeline short is to have the design, engineering, and site plan fully locked before anything goes to the HOA or the city — which is exactly the kind of groundwork we handle before a shovel ever moves.
